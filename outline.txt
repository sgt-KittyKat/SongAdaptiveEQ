Yes, this is absolutely doable, and I think it has real potential as a hackathon project. The interesting part isn't building an equalizer. It's building a system that automatically determines how a particular song should be equalized for a particular audio device.

There is already technology that solves parts of this problem, and I found one open-source project that comes surprisingly close to your exact idea.

My recommendation would be to build a device-aware, song-adaptive EQ engine, initially as a web application, and demonstrate how the same song gets different EQ settings on different headphones.

A. Analyze the song
You can analyze a song mathematically without training an AI model.

One of the key techniques is the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), which converts an audio signal into a representation of its frequency components.

From that, you can calculate:
Feature	What you learn
Spectral centroid	How bright or dark the recording is
Band energy	How much energy exists in the bass, mids and treble
Spectral contrast	How prominent peaks and valleys are across frequency bands
RMS / loudness	How strong the signal is
Dynamic range / crest factor	How strongly transients stand out
Spectral variation over time	How the frequency balance changes between sections

For example, your system might detect that a recording has unusually prominent upper-mid frequencies, suggesting that a gentle reduction could be worth testing.

However, here's a crucial distinction: a song with lots of bass isn't necessarily a song with too much bass. That might be exactly how the artist intended it to sound.

You should therefore compare tonal characteristics against appropriate references, or identify unusually narrow spectral imbalances while preserving the song's overall tonal signature.
B. Analyze the output device
This is arguably the most straightforward part.

Different headphones produce different frequency responses. One model might exaggerate frequencies around 100 Hz while another might have a prominent peak around 6 kHz.

You want to compensate for these differences.

A simple expression is:
\[
EQ_{\text{device}}(f)=T(f)-H(f)
\]

Where:
- \(T(f)\) is your desired target response.
- \(H(f)\) is the headphone's measured frequency response relative to that reference.
- The result is the correction in decibels at frequency \(f\).
You don't have to build a measurement database from scratch.

Use AutoEq on GitHub.

It provides headphone frequency-response measurements, target curves, and precomputed EQ settings. You should check the licensing and attribution requirements for the specific measurement datasets, not just the software.

One important caveat: frequency-response correction does not tell you everything about a device's physical limits. A tiny phone speaker cannot suddenly reproduce deep sub-bass just because you boost 30 Hz. True capability-aware optimization would need information about distortion and maximum output as well.
C. Generate the combined EQ
A simplified mathematical formulation would be:
\[
\boxed{EQ_{\text{final}}(f)=EQ_{\text{device}}(f)+\alpha EQ_{\text{song}}(f)+EQ_{\text{taste}}(f)}
\]

Where \(\alpha\) controls how strongly your algorithm modifies the song itself.

For a first version, I would make the song correction relatively conservative, for example limiting it to around ±2–3 dB, while letting the device correction handle larger measured deviations where appropriate.

Then convert your desired EQ curve into something that can actually be applied, such as 5–10 parametric EQ filters.

You'll also need headroom management to prevent clipping, smooth filter transitions, and protection against extreme boosts.

The development phases
1. Build an audio player. Upload a WAV or MP3, play it directly in a browser, and apply a controllable equalizer.
2. Add headphone profiles. Include perhaps 5–10 popular headphone models using existing measurement data. The user selects their model.
3. Analyze the recording. Calculate its spectral distribution and dynamics using FFT-based processing.
4. Implement automatic correction. Combine headphone correction with a modest, explainable song-dependent adjustment. Start with static per-song EQ rather than constantly changing filters.

MDN+1

For example, applying a generated set of filters in the browser could look like this:

const ctx = new AudioContext();

const audio = document.querySelector("audio");
const source = ctx.createMediaElementSource(audio);

// Example filters — not recommended values
const eqSettings = [
  { type: "lowshelf", frequency: 100, gain: -2 },
  { type: "peaking", frequency: 3000, Q: 1.2, gain: 1.5 },
  { type: "highshelf", frequency: 8500, gain: -1 }
];

const preamp = ctx.createGain();
preamp.gain.value = Math.pow(10, -4 / 20);

source.connect(preamp);

let previous = preamp;

for (const band of eqSettings) {
  const filter = ctx.createBiquadFilter();

  filter.type = band.type;
  filter.frequency.value = band.frequency;
  filter.Q.value = band.Q ?? 1;
  filter.gain.value = band.gain;

  previous.connect(filter);
  previous = filter;
}

previous.connect(ctx.destination);

// Resume ctx from a user click before playback.



This implements the audio-processing part. Your actual innovation would be the function that produces eqSettings from the track analysis and selected device profile.

I would also avoid adding a large language model just to make the project seem AI-powered. A good signal-processing algorithm is more appropriate initially. Machine learning becomes valuable later if you want the system to learn individual listening preferences.
5. The two biggest technical challenges
There is no objectively perfect EQ for every song
Suppose a metal recording has aggressive guitars around 2–5 kHz.

Your algorithm might interpret that as excess upper-mid energy and reduce it. But doing so could remove the exact character that makes the recording exciting.

The correct approach is not to flatten every song's frequency spectrum.

A better strategy is to establish a neutral device baseline, preserve the recording's overall frequency balance, and apply only conservative, justifiable song-dependent corrections.

Eventually, you could train a personalization model using users' A/B listening preferences, rather than pretending the FFT alone can determine what sounds best.
You cannot freely modify audio from every application
This matters for the product vision.

Spotify's developer policies restrict altering Spotify content, so a Spotify Web Playback SDK integration is not an appropriate shortcut for this particular use case. 

Spotify for Developers+1

iOS also generally prevents third-party applications from applying custom EQ to every other app's audio.

For the hackathon, processing audio inside your own player avoids much of this complexity. Later, desktop system-wide audio processing is a more realistic next step than universal iOS integration.
6. How I would make the project stand out
Given that Leungo already combines headphone compensation and song-adaptive processing, your strongest competitive advantage could be demonstrable personalization that actually improves listener preference.

Imagine this:

Someone uploads a song, selects their headphones, and the system generates a suggested EQ. The listener hears two loudness-matched versions without knowing which is which and selects the one they prefer.

Over repeated comparisons, the application learns their preferred tonal balance.

That gives you a meaningful long-term direction:

V1: Device-aware automatic EQ

V2: Per-song adaptive EQ

V3: Personalized EQ learned from listening preferences

V4: Context-aware EQ accounting for output volume, room acoustics, and device constraints

The pitch could be:
An intelligent audio engine that adapts music to your headphones, your listening preferences, and the characteristics of each recording — automatically.

My recommendation
For the hackathon, focus on making the algorithm work and proving that it changes the sound intelligently, rather than trying to support every playback device.

Your strongest demonstration would show two contrasting tracks, two headphone models, the automatically generated EQ curves, and genuine before/after playback. Then let the judges compare the results themselves.

The fact that much of the device calibration and DSP infrastructure already exists means you can spend the hackathon working on the genuinely interesting part: deciding what changes should be made to a song and whether those changes actually sound better.

One important question that would influence the architecture: are you participating in a 24-hour, 48-hour, or longer hackathon? That determines whether I'd prioritize a browser-based prototype or a more ambitious desktop audio engine.
